A component has exactly one story. Everything else you see across the tabs of
that story is a different **kind of answer to a different question**, which is
why they are not all the same thing and why one story full of examples would be
worse than what is here.

| Tab | Written as | Answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Preview | the story's `render`, or its `component` | What does this do when I change a prop? |
| A scene | `scenes: [{ name, render }]` | What does it look like arranged THIS way? |
| A scene | `scenes: [{ name, example }]` | Here is a composition worth copying. |
| A demo | a `*.demo.tsx` file beside the component | Here is a whole worked screen. |
| Docs | `docs`, `usage`, `guidelines`, `*.docs.mdx` | Why does it exist and when do I reach for it? |

Only the preview and a `render` scene are driven by the Controls tab. That is
the whole distinction, and everything below follows from it.

## `render` or `example`

A scene is written one of two ways, and writing both is a type error.

**`render` reads the live args**, so the controls keep driving it. Use it when
the scene is the same component under a different arrangement and a reader would
still want to push its props around: a row inside a group, a card at three
widths, a control in an error state.

```tsx
scenes: [
  {
    name: 'A list',
    render: ({ icon, label }) => (
      <Box gap={10}>
        <ListRow.Root icon={icon}>
          <ListRow.Label>{label}</ListRow.Label>
        </ListRow.Root>
        <ListRow.Root icon="language">
          <ListRow.Label>Audio</ListRow.Label>
        </ListRow.Root>
      </Box>
    ),
  },
]
```

**`example` is a fixed composition**, and the controls step aside rather than
sitting there moving nothing. Use it when the point of the scene is the
arrangement itself and the args would be meaningless against it: media at the
head of a row, a group of three specific members, a shape assembled from parts
the story's args never mention.

```tsx
scenes: [
  {
    name: 'Media at the head',
    example: () => (
      <ListRow.Root>
        <ListRow.Leading>
          <Avatar name="Maxime" size={34} circle />
        </ListRow.Leading>
        <ListRow.Label>Maxime</ListRow.Label>
      </ListRow.Root>
    ),
  },
]
```

**Writing neither** reuses the story's own render with the scene's `args` merged
in, which is the shortest way to say "the same thing, with these values".

The test is not how complicated the markup is. It is: *would a reader want the
controls to reach this?* If yes, `render`. If the controls would be dead
furniture beside it, `example`.

## A scene or a demo

A scene lives inside the story file and shows the component. A demo is a whole
`*.demo.tsx` file beside the component, discovered by its name, and shows a
screen built out of it: the surrounding layout, the state, the handlers, the
other components it sits with.

Reach for a demo when the thing worth showing is bigger than the component: a
sign-up form rather than a field, a settings panel rather than a row. A demo
opens with its source already showing, because its whole purpose is to be read
and copied. A scene is a take on the component; a demo is a use of it.

## Why not one story with a dozen examples

Because the controls would have nothing to drive, and the Props and Interactions
tabs would have no single subject. The story is the unit that carries a
component's identity: its variants, its args, its props table, its git history,
its link to source. A page of unrelated examples is a page, and this workbench
has those too, written as `*.page.mdx` and filed under Guides.

So: one story per component, scenes for takes on it, demos for screens built out
of it, and a page when the subject is not a component at all.
